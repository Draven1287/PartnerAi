Chapter 05 · outline

Build with AI.

Pick a project. Finish it in a weekend. Then pick a bigger one. This is where the real learning happens.

Early Access Preview

This chapter is a project starter map. Full walkthroughs are still coming, but each project below now includes a finish line and a starter prompt so you can begin today.

Why "make something" matters

You can read about AI forever and still not be good at it. The only way to actually develop the partner skill is to use it to build things you couldn't have built without it. Here are five starter projects, ordered roughly easiest → hardest.

Project 1 · Your personal tutor

Goal: Build a custom study tutor for a subject you're learning. Use AI to quiz you, explain weak spots, and adapt to your level.

Done = a tutor prompt that teaches one unit, quizzes you, reacts to a wrong answer, and gives you one next practice step.

Act as my study tutor for [SUBJECT].
First ask what I already know.
Then teach one concept, quiz me with 3 questions,
and adapt if I get one wrong.
Do not give final answers until I try.

Why it's a great starter: Pure prompting, no code, immediate payoff. Most people who try this stop using textbooks.

Project 2 · A useful tool for your own life

Goal: Pick a small annoying problem in your life, like a packing checklist, a recipe converter, or a study schedule generator, and build a working tool for it with AI.

Done = one reusable checklist, calculator, plan, or mini webpage that solves a real repeated problem for you.

Help me turn this repeated problem into a tiny useful tool:
[DESCRIBE PROBLEM]
Ask me 5 questions first.
Then propose the simplest version I can build today.

Why it matters: Forces you to translate a vague human need into precise instructions. That translation skill is the AI partner skill.

Project 3 · A 30-minute deep-dive

Goal: Pick a complex topic you know nothing about. In 30 minutes, use AI to take you from zero to "I can explain this to a friend." Then explain it to a friend.

Done = a one-page explanation in your own words, three things you verified outside AI, and one question you still have.

Teach me [TOPIC] from zero.
Use plain language, one analogy, and a 30-minute plan.
After each section, ask me to explain it back.

Why it matters: Practices the "just-in-time learning" move that AI uniquely enables.

Project 4 · Stress-test the AI

Goal: Pick a topic you already know well (a sport, a book, a hobby). Find where the AI gets it wrong. Document the failure modes.

Done = a short failure report with three AI mistakes, why they happened, and how a learner could catch them.

I know a lot about [TOPIC].
Answer these 5 hard questions.
Then I will mark what you got wrong.
After that, help me classify the failure modes.

Why it matters: Builds the most important defensive skill: knowing when to trust AI and when to verify.

Project 5 · Fine-tune a small language model

Goal: Fine-tune an open-weight or source-available language model on a laptop. Yes, really.

This sounds impossible. It's not. The author of this site did a small fine-tuning run on a MacBook Air in about 4 minutes for $0 in local compute cost. The trick is simple: don't train from scratch. Start with an existing model, check its license, and fine-tune on a small dataset.

What you'll need: Usually an Apple Silicon Mac or a computer with enough RAM/VRAM for the model you choose, a framework such as Apple MLX or Hugging Face TRL, a dataset, and time to test the result.

Done = a documented local-model experiment: model name, dataset, training method, before/after output, and what changed.

Help me design a beginner-safe fine-tuning experiment.
I have [COMPUTER] and want the model to improve at [TASK].
List the smallest setup, risks, and what result would prove it worked.

Why this matters: Once you've fine-tuned a model with your own hands, you'll never see AI as magic again. You'll understand more of the machine. That changes how you talk to it.

→ See Aarav's project for an example

Tool note: model licenses, hardware requirements, and free tiers vary. Check the model card, license, and tool documentation before assuming a model is free, open source, or commercially usable. Useful starting points: Meta Llama, Hugging Face model cards, and Apple MLX.
