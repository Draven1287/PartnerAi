# LearningAI course authoring standard

## Decision source

This standard applies the recurring findings from seven teen-persona reviews to all 50 lessons. It does not optimize for one persona or for the loudest response. It keeps directions supported by the majority and preserves important safety/accessibility needs even when they came from a smaller group.

## Course promise

LearningAI helps a learner use AI without surrendering privacy, voice, attention, verification, or the final human decision. Progress means demonstrated capability—not compliance, time spent, or clicking Next.

## Required lesson rhythm

Every lesson must:

1. Open with a concrete consequence, task, or tension before naming the mechanism.
2. Make clear why the lesson matters in ordinary life.
3. Include several meaningful learner decisions—not one obvious virtue answer.
4. Give immediate explanatory feedback and allow retry without shame.
5. Include at least two gated learner actions before one final `exitCheck`.
6. End with transfer to a fresh scenario rather than recall of the wording.
7. Keep `toolkitSave` optional; Saved Notes never gates Next.
8. Avoid requesting real names, schools, addresses, account numbers, private messages, health details, passwords, or another person's data.
9. Offer activities that work for learners who are not college-bound and are not office workers. Across every arc, mix school, work, family, creative, social, practical, and community contexts.
10. Preserve stable IDs (`chapter-N`), the ten-arc map, and the existing V2 lesson engine.

## Decision quality

- A decision must change an outcome, protect something, expose a tradeoff, or choose evidence.
- Distractors should be plausible mistakes, not silly or morally evil answers.
- “Ask the AI again” is not independent verification.
- Confidence, polish, repetition, and citations generated by the same model are not proof.
- The learner should sometimes choose among several useful options and explain which constraint matters.

## Gradual progression

### Arc 1 — First Contact

Start safe, identify the system, use AI once, understand prediction, then verify and decide.

### Arc 2 — How It Works

Build a plain-language model of generation, uncertainty, products, modalities, and technical vocabulary without pretending a chatbot is a mind.

### Arc 3 — Talking to AI

Move from a real goal to context, follow-ups, privacy, and human ownership.

### Arc 4 — Trust & Everyday AI

Build repeatable habits for checking, memory, dependence, embedded AI, tools, and agents.

### Arc 5 — Prompting Craft

Practice audience, constraints, examples, coaching, and checkable outputs.

### Arc 6 — Judgment & Safety

Practice hallucination checks, bias, scams, provenance, and when not to use AI.

### Arc 7 — AI & Being Human

Protect relationships, feelings, attention, learning effort, authorship, and honest disclosure.

### Arc 8 — AI for Real Life

Apply the controls to everyday tasks, money, travel, health, thinking, and revision.

### Arc 9 — Building with AI

Plan, delegate, preserve voice, and turn one-off prompts into human-approved workflows.

### Arc 10 — Becoming a Builder

Work with permissions, build evidence, teach the controls, examine consequences, and leave with a personal operating system.

## External AI rule

Live-tool use may be offered when it is genuinely useful, but the lesson must first teach redaction and the decision boundary. It must say when data leaves LearningAI. An external assistant must not be the only way to understand the concept. Built-in sorting, repair, workflow, verification, comparison, or design activities must carry the required learning evidence.

## Completion evidence

Each lesson should leave one small proof: a sorted decision set, repaired instruction, verification trail, ordered workflow, selected permission boundary, or transferable exit decision. Optional Saved Notes can capture that proof, but saving remains optional.

## Evidence foundation

Course-wide safety and judgment decisions should remain consistent with these primary sources:

- UNESCO, **Guidance for generative AI in education and research**: human-centred and age-appropriate use, data-privacy protection, and ethical/pedagogical validation — https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research
- UNESCO, **AI and education: Protecting the rights of learners**: rights-based access, inclusion, privacy, safety, accountability, and equitable benefit — https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-and-education-protecting-rights-learners
- NIST, **AI Risk Management Framework and Generative AI Profile**: testing, evaluation, verification, validation, and risk management tied to goals and context — https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework
- U.S. Federal Trade Commission, **Fake emergency scams**: voice cloning, urgency, secrecy, emotional pressure, and independent family verification — https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/scammers-use-fake-emergencies-steal-your-money

These sources set the floor. Individual lessons that make specific historical, medical, legal, financial, environmental, or fast-changing technical claims still need their own current authoritative sources.
